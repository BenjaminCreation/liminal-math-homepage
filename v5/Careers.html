Introduction

A mathematics degree is a licence, not a job title

mathematics plus one
IntroductionBefore you choose

During the 2024–25 placement season, a computer science student at IIT Madras accepted a pre-placement offer from the trading firm Jane Street reported at ₹4.3 crore a year — salary, bonus and relocation included — for a role in Hong Kong. The figure made headlines for a week. What the headlines missed was the more useful fact underneath it: the firm making that offer does not hire for a branch, a degree title or a college name. It hires for one thing, and it tests for that thing with puzzles a strong olympiad student would recognise on sight.

That quiet shift is what this atlas is about. For most of the last century, the honest answer to “what does a mathematics student become?” was short — a teacher, a professor, perhaps a statistician in a government office. In 2026 the answer is long, and parts of it are startling. Mathematics now sits underneath trading desks, AI laboratories, cryptographic standards, insurance pricing, clinical trials, logistics networks and the software running on every phone in the country.

So the first idea to unlearn is that a mathematics degree is a job title. It is not. It is a licence — proof that you can enter an unfamiliar formal system, learn its rules faster than most people, and reason inside it without a guide. Employers are not buying the theorems you memorised. They are buying the speed at which you will learn their problem.

The conventional wisdom in many Indian households runs the other way: pure mathematics is the narrow, risky choice; engineering is the broad, safe one. An honest answer has to face the strongest version of that worry, and it is a real one. A BSc in mathematics, on its own, attracts fewer campus recruiters than a B.Tech from a comparable institution, and a student who stops at “I am good at maths” will find the market politely uninterested. But the conclusion people draw from that — avoid mathematics — is close to the opposite of the right one. Skills tied to today’s tools depreciate when the tools change, and this decade has shown how fast that can happen. Mathematics is the one asset in the portfolio that does not.

What the market actually rewards is mathematics plus one: mathematics plus programming, plus statistics, plus finance, plus the ability to explain. Almost every page that follows lands on that formula. The careers differ only in which “one” they demand.

Door two: employersHiring

The interview at a top trading firm is not a finance exam. It is an olympiad round with money in the examples. That is not a figure of speech: Jane Street, Citadel and OpenAI are the IMO’s main sponsors, Citadel takes on dozens of olympiad competitors as interns every year, and many of Hudson River Trading’s earliest interns already knew each other as teenagers from the olympiad circuit. Quant firms now send people to high-school mathematics tournaments the way football clubs send scouts to academy matches.

Count where medallists end up and the picture sharpens. One tally of firms employing international olympiad medallists placed Jane Street fourth — behind only Google, Meta and Google DeepMind. Read that list again: two AI labs, a technology giant and a trading firm. The modern market for mathematical talent is, in large part, a contest between finance and artificial intelligence.

Door three: a lifetime4,710 participants

The best long-run evidence comes from economists Ruchir Agarwal and Patrick Gaule, who traced 4,710 IMO participants from 1981 to 2000.

22%went on to a mathematics PhD
+2.6%more publications per extra point scored (out of 42)
+4.5%more citations per extra point
~50×as likely to win a Fields Medal as a top-ten PhD graduate (IMO gold)

The finding that matters most for India sits in the second half of their paper. Participants from low-income countries produced about 35% fewer mathematics publications and over 50% fewer citations than equally talented participants from rich countries — and the gap came largely from not going on to a PhD at all. The penalty was not talent. It was access. Strip away the medals and this is a market-design problem: ability is spread fairly evenly across the world; the routes out of it are not.

What a medal does not buyThe honest part

Here the atlas has to be honest, because parents rarely are. Roughly four in five IMO participants never earn a mathematics PhD. Contest mathematics and research mathematics are different sports: an olympiad problem is designed to have a clean solution reachable in ninety minutes; a research problem may have no solution, and nobody knows in advance. A medal is a signal with an expiry date — it opens the first interview and the first admission, and by your second job nobody asks.

And most students who climb the ladder never reach the camp at the top. That is fine. The habits the climb builds — patience with a hard problem, proof as a standard of honesty, the instinct to test a claim before trusting it — transfer to every career in this atlas. The medal is the receipt. The training is the purchase.

Part III · A plan by stagestart where you are

Choosing: a plan by stage.

An atlas is useful only if it changes what you do on Monday. What follows is not a script — every student’s path bends — but a sequence that keeps the most doors open for the longest time.

  1. 1
    Classes 8–10Build the habit

    Learn to write a proof, not just reach an answer. Attempt the IOQM early, for the experience rather than the result. Start one programming language. Read mathematics that is not on the syllabus.

  2. 2
    Classes 11–12Climb, but do not drill

    Take the olympiad ladder seriously, and know the admission routes it opens — CMI, ISI, IIT Bombay, IIT Kanpur, IIT Madras and IIT Kharagpur all reward olympiad performance in different ways. Resist the temptation to replace understanding with volume. Drilling buys pattern recognition that fades; understanding compounds.

  3. 3
    Undergraduate years 1–2The core plus one

    Make analysis, algebra, linear algebra and probability rock-solid — every career in this atlas leans on them. Choose your “plus one” early: programming, statistics, economics or finance. Take one course outside your comfort zone each year.

  4. 4
    Undergraduate years 3–4Test the waters

    Do at least one research project and one industry internship, ideally in different directions. You learn more from discovering what you dislike than from a brochure. Decide between a PhD track (NBHM, CSIR-NET, applications abroad) and industry by the start of your final year.

  5. 5
    After the degreeSpecialise deliberately

    Specialisation is where pay and satisfaction diverge, so choose with both in view. A master’s or PhD should be a decision, not a default. Whichever path you take, keep your mathematics alive — it is the part of your training that will still be worth something in 2040.
